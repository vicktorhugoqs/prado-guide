Before the Prado: a spoken introduction

About half an hour, written to be listened to. Select your browser's read-aloud and put the phone in your pocket. The written guide, with a room number for every painting, is for the galleries themselves.

This is a talk to listen to before you go in, not in front of the paintings. It gives you the background that the labels on the walls take for granted: who bought these pictures, why they are in Madrid and not somewhere else, and what to look for when you are standing in front of them. There are twelve short chapters. If you only have ten minutes, listen to the first two and the one on Velázquez.

One. A king's collection, not a museum's

Most great museums were assembled by curators trying to tell the whole story of art. The Prado was not. It is, at heart, the Spanish royal collection: paintings bought, commissioned, inherited and now and then looted by the kings of Spain over three centuries, and opened to the public in November 1819.

That one fact explains almost everything you will notice today. It explains the depth. Where most museums have one or two pictures by a great painter, the Prado has some ninety by Rubens, around fifty by Velázquez, more than thirty by Titian, and well over a hundred by Goya. The kings did not collect a sample of everyone. They bought what they loved, and they bought a great deal of it.

It also explains the gaps. Dutch painting of the golden age is thin, a single Rembrandt and no Vermeer at all, because the Dutch spent eighty years in rebellion against the Spanish crown. There are no French Impressionists. The kings bought from the lands they ruled or wanted to rule, Flanders, Naples, Milan, and from the painters who worked for them. So the Prado is not a survey. It is a portrait of a dynasty's taste.

The habit begins with Isabella of Castile, at the end of the fifteenth century, who loved the devotional panels of Flanders. Her grandson Charles the Fifth, who ruled Spain, the Netherlands, much of Italy, the Holy Roman Empire and the new American territories, found his painter in Titian. The story goes that the emperor once stooped to pick up a brush Titian had dropped, saying that Titian deserved to be served by Caesar. It is almost certainly a legend, but it tells you how a painter's status was changing.

Charles's son, Philip the Second, made Madrid the capital in 1561 and built the vast monastery-palace of El Escorial in the mountains to the north. He was austere, devout, a workaholic bureaucrat, and he collected two painters who could hardly be more different: the Venetian Titian, whose sensuous mythologies he commissioned, and the Flemish Hieronymus Bosch, whose nightmares he hunted down across Europe.

Then comes the greatest collector of them all, Philip the Fourth, who reigned from 1621 to 1665. He kept Velázquez at his side for almost forty years. He was Rubens's biggest client. When the English executed Charles the First and sold his collection, Philip's ambassador in London bought heavily. When Rubens died, Philip bought from his estate. And all this happened while Spain itself was losing wars, losing Portugal, and going bankrupt. That is the great paradox of the Spanish seventeenth century: political decline and a golden age of painting at exactly the same time.

The last Habsburg king, the sickly Charles the Second, died without children in 1700, and the throne passed to a French Bourbon, Philip the Fifth, grandson of Louis the Fourteenth. On Christmas Eve 1734 the old royal palace in Madrid, the Alcázar, burned down, and hundreds of paintings were lost. Las Meninas survived. The Bourbons built the Royal Palace you can visit today on the same site, and later in the century Charles the Third commissioned the long neoclassical building you are about to enter, designed by Juan de Villanueva, originally as a museum of natural history.

Then Napoleon. In 1808 French troops occupied Spain and Napoleon put his brother Joseph on the throne. Paintings were seized, sold and carried off. When Joseph fled in 1813 his baggage train was captured by the Duke of Wellington at the battle of Vitoria, and some of the royal pictures that were in it still hang in Wellington's London house, Apsley House, because the restored Spanish king let him keep them.

That king, Ferdinand the Seventh, was no friend of liberty, but he did one good thing. Encouraged, it is usually said, by his Portuguese wife, Maria Isabel of Braganza, he opened the Villanueva building as a royal museum of paintings on the nineteenth of November 1819, with about three hundred Spanish pictures on show. The rest followed over the next decades. After the revolution of 1868 it became a national museum. During the Civil War, from 1936, its masterpieces were evacuated, first to Valencia, then to Catalonia, and finally, in 1939, to Geneva, where they were shown to the public before returning home as the Second World War began.

Two. The building, and how to spend the afternoon

The Villanueva building runs north to south along the Paseo del Prado. Three bronze painters stand guard outside: Goya at the north end, Velázquez in front of the main façade, and Murillo at the south end, facing the Botanical Garden. Behind it, Rafael Moneo's extension of 2007 wraps around the old cloister of the church of San Jerónimo, and holds the ticket hall, the shop, the café and the temporary exhibitions.

There are three floors, and the numbering is exactly the British one. The plan calls the ground floor floor nought. The first floor is the main floor, with Velázquez, El Greco, Titian, Rubens and Goya's royal portraits. The second floor is the top, quieter and smaller, with Goya's tapestry designs and the Dutch and Flemish cabinet pictures. Pick up the free paper plan as you go in. Room numbers matter here: the building is a long sequence of connected galleries, and it is easy to walk in circles.

There is no fixed circuit. The written guide suggests one: start on the ground floor at the north end with the oldest pictures, the Flemish painters and Bosch, go up to the first floor for the Venetians, El Greco and Velázquez, carry on to Rubens and Goya at court, climb to the top floor if you still have the energy, and finish back on the ground floor with Goya's war paintings and the Black Paintings. Ending with Goya is a good idea. Nothing else in the building can follow it.

A few practical things. Big bags and backpacks go to the free cloakroom. Photography is not allowed in the galleries, and the guards enforce it, which is a blessing: people actually look. The last two hours of every day are free, so from six o'clock, or five on Sundays, the museum fills up. And plan to sit down at least twice. Most people's attention gives out after an hour and a half or so, long before the paintings do. A bench in front of one great picture is worth more than another twenty pictures walked past.

Three. The Flemish painters, and the strange case of Bosch

The oldest pictures on the ground floor come from the Low Countries in the fifteenth century, and the first thing to notice is the paint itself. Flemish painters perfected oil painting: thin, transparent layers of colour laid one over another, like sheets of tinted glass. The light goes into the paint and comes back out of it. That is why a fold of blue cloth or a tear on a cheek in these pictures can look more real than reality.

The great example is Rogier van der Weyden's Descent from the Cross, painted before 1443 for a guild of crossbowmen in the town of Leuven. Ten figures, nearly life size, are packed into a shallow golden box, like painted sculpture. Look at the Virgin. She has fainted, and her body falls in exactly the same curve as her dead son's. The idea is called compassio: she suffers the Passion with him. Look for the tears, painted one by one, and for the little crossbows hidden in the corners.

Next door are the paintings of Hieronymus Bosch, and they raise an obvious question. Why would Philip the Second, the most devout Catholic monarch in Europe, fill his monastery with these crowded, obscene, hallucinatory scenes? The answer, as the librarian of El Escorial explained at the time, is that Bosch was a moralist. His pictures are sermons. Other painters, the librarian wrote, paint people as they wish to appear; Bosch dared to paint them as they are inside.

The Garden of Earthly Delights is the most famous. Read it like a book, from left to right. On the left, God presents Eve to Adam in paradise. In the centre, a whole world of naked men and women gives itself up to pleasure, eating giant fruit, riding in circles, hiding inside bubbles and shells. On the right is hell, in darkness, where musical instruments have become instruments of torture. Do not try to decode everything. Nobody can. Choose one corner and look at it properly, then another. Scholars still argue about what it means, and that is part of its power.

Nearby, look at Bruegel's Triumph of Death, where armies of skeletons sweep across a burnt landscape and spare no one, from king to peasant; and at Joachim Patinir's small, blue Crossing of the Styx, where the ferryman of the dead rows a single soul between paradise on one bank and hell on the other. In the next room is Albrecht Dürer, who painted himself at twenty-six dressed as a gentleman, with fine kid gloves, at a time when a painter in Germany was still considered a craftsman. It is a declaration of independence in the form of a portrait.

Four. Italy, Titian, and the argument about colour

Before you climb the stairs to Titian, it helps to know about a quarrel that ran through the whole Renaissance. In Florence and Rome, artists believed that the essence of painting was disegno, drawing: the clear outline, the planned composition, the idea. In Venice, painters believed in colorito: colour, light, and the texture of paint laid directly on the canvas. The writer Vasari tells us that Michelangelo, visiting Titian, praised his colour, and then remarked afterwards that it was a pity the Venetians had never learnt to draw.

The Spanish kings, on the whole, sided with Venice. That decision shaped everything that happened next in Spanish painting, because Velázquez spent his working life surrounded by Titians, and Rubens came to Madrid and copied them.

On the ground floor you will have seen the Florentine side of the argument, in Fra Angelico's Annunciation, with its measured portico and its golden ray of light, and in Raphael's portrait of a cardinal. Upstairs, in the long Central Gallery, is Titian's portrait of Charles the Fifth on horseback at the battle of Mühlberg, painted in 1548. There is no battle in it. Just one man, one horse, one lance, at dusk. It became the model for every royal portrait on horseback that followed.

In the rooms of Venetian painting, look for the mythologies Titian painted for Philip the Second, which he called poesie, poems in paint. In his late work, Titian applied the paint so loosely, it was said, that he used his fingers as much as his brushes. Stand close to his Danaë and you will see why the younger painters who came after him were so excited.

Five. El Greco, the outsider

Domenikos Theotokopoulos was born in Crete, then a Venetian possession, and trained there as a painter of Orthodox icons. He went to Venice, where he studied Titian, and to Rome, where he is said to have offered to repaint Michelangelo's Last Judgement, which did not make him popular. In the 1570s he came to Spain, hoping to work for Philip the Second at El Escorial. The king did not like the altarpiece he ordered from him. So the Greek, as everyone called him, stayed in Toledo for the rest of his life, nearly forty years, and became the most original painter of his age.

When you see his work, notice three things. The bodies stretch upwards, longer than life, as if they were flames. The colours are sharp and acid, yellows, pinks and greens that nobody else used. And the light often seems to come from inside the figures rather than from the sky. His late Adoration of the Shepherds, painted for his own tomb, is lit only by the newborn child. For two centuries after his death El Greco was thought eccentric, even mad. Then, around 1900, modern painters rediscovered him, and suddenly he looked like the future.

Before the strange late pictures, look at his early portrait of an unknown nobleman with his hand on his chest. Black clothes, a white ruff, a long thin hand, a sword hilt in the corner. It has become the image of the Spanish gentleman, and it was painted by a Greek.

Six. Caravaggio's shadow: Naples and Seville

Around 1600 in Rome, Caravaggio changed European painting. He painted saints with dirty feet, using ordinary people as models, and he lit them like a stage: one strong beam of light, and darkness everywhere else. The Prado has only one Caravaggio, a young David tying the hair of Goliath's severed head, but it has many of the painters he transformed.

The route ran through Naples, which was ruled by Spain. Jusepe de Ribera, born near Valencia, spent his whole career there, painting martyrs and philosophers with wrinkled skin and real weight. His Martyrdom of Saint Philip, with workmen straining at ropes to hoist the saint, is not about agony so much as about labour.

The other great centre was Seville, the richest city in Spain, because every ship from the Americas had to come through it. Its convents and churches commissioned paintings by the hundred. Francisco de Zurbarán painted monks in white habits with a stillness that is almost mystical, and he could give four clay pots on a shelf the same seriousness as a saint. Bartolomé Esteban Murillo painted softer, sweeter pictures, above all the Immaculate Conception: the Virgin as a young girl floating on the moon, carried up by a crowd of child angels. Seville was the heartland of that devotion, and Murillo gave it its definitive image.

Velázquez was born in Seville too, and he started out painting exactly this kind of naturalism: kitchens, water-sellers, real faces in a raking light. Which brings us to the heart of the museum.

Seven. Velázquez, the painter of painters

Diego Velázquez was born in Seville in 1599 and trained with the painter Francisco Pacheco, whose daughter he married. In 1623, at twenty-four, he painted a portrait of the young Philip the Fourth that so pleased the king that he was made a court painter on the spot. He never left the king's service. He went to Italy twice, in 1629 and again in 1649, and each time came back changed. He rose steadily as a courtier, until he was in charge of the royal palaces themselves, and in 1659 he was made a knight of the Order of Santiago, the ambition of his life. He died the following year, exhausted after organising the ceremonies for the marriage of the king's daughter to Louis the Fourteenth.

Because he worked for one patron, almost all his major paintings stayed in the royal collection, and so almost all of them are here. When the French painter Édouard Manet came to the Prado in 1865, he wrote home that Velázquez was the painter of painters, and that the trip had been worth it for him alone.

The key to looking at Velázquez is distance. Walk right up to one of his late paintings and you will see almost nothing: loose, broken strokes, a few touches of white, smears of grey. Then step back three or four metres, and it all resolves into silk, lace, flesh and air. A phrase often quoted from his contemporary, the poet Quevedo, says he painted with distant blots. Do this with every Velázquez you see: close, then far. It is the single most rewarding thing you can do in the building.

Follow his career through the rooms. The Triumph of Bacchus, known in Madrid simply as The Drunks, puts the pale god of wine among real, leathery, grinning Spanish peasants. Vulcan's Forge, painted in Rome, catches the moment after bad news: Apollo has just told the blacksmith god that his wife is betraying him, and you can watch the news travel around the room, face by face. The Surrender of Breda turns a military victory into an act of courtesy, the Spanish general stopping his defeated enemy from kneeling, with the forest of upright lances behind him. And the little prince Baltasar Carlos rides his pony straight towards you, against the mountains north of Madrid. He was the great hope of the dynasty, and he died at sixteen.

Then Las Meninas, in Room twelve, painted in 1656. Take your time. Stand first in front of it, then in the middle of the room. At the centre is the five-year-old princess, the Infanta Margarita, surrounded by her maids of honour, who are the meninas of the title, two court dwarfs, a sleepy dog, and a chaperone. At the left, behind an enormous canvas, stands Velázquez himself, brush in hand, looking out. At you. On the back wall is a mirror, and in the mirror are the king and queen. Which means that they are standing where you are standing. You are in the king's place, and everyone in the picture has just noticed you.

What exactly is Velázquez painting on that huge canvas? A portrait of the king and queen? This very picture? Nobody knows, and the painting has been argued over ever since. The Neapolitan painter Luca Giordano called it the theology of painting. In 1957 Picasso painted fifty-eight variations on it. And the philosopher Michel Foucault opened his book The Order of Things, in 1966, with a long reading of it, as a picture about representation itself, about the place of the one who sees and the one who is seen. Notice, too, the red cross on Velázquez's chest. That is the Order of Santiago, which he only received in 1659, three years after the picture was finished. It was added later. Legend says the king painted it in himself.

Close to Las Meninas, find The Spinners. At first sight it is a scene in a tapestry workshop, with women spinning wool in the foreground. But look at the brightly lit alcove at the back. There, the goddess Athena, in her helmet, is confronting Arachne, the mortal weaver who dared to challenge her, and the tapestry behind them copies a Titian that hung in the king's palace. It is a painting about art, rivalry and copying. And look at the spinning wheel on the left: Velázquez has painted it in motion, the spokes a blur. Two centuries before photography.

Eight. Rubens, the painter-diplomat

Peter Paul Rubens was the most successful painter in Europe, the head of a huge workshop in Antwerp, a scholar fluent in several languages, and a diplomat who helped negotiate peace between Spain and England. In 1628 he came to Madrid on a diplomatic mission and stayed for eight months. He spent his spare time in the palace copying Titian, and he spent time with the young Velázquez, who was twenty-two years younger. Rubens's energy, and his advice to go to Italy, shaped Velázquez's next decade.

The Rubens rooms are about abundance: flesh, colour, movement, life. The Three Graces was a painting he kept for himself and never sold. The Garden of Love celebrates his second marriage, at fifty-three, to sixteen-year-old Helena Fourment. Philip the Fourth bought both from his estate. It is worth knowing that for a time in the nineteenth century, the museum's nudes, Rubens's and Titian's among them, were kept in a locked room that could only be visited with special permission. Tastes change. These pictures were always here.

Nine. Goya, from the court to the dark

Francisco de Goya's life runs almost exactly across the collapse of the old Spain, and you can read that collapse in his work.

He was born in 1746 in a village in Aragon, and came to Madrid in his late twenties to design tapestries for the royal factory: bright, fashionable scenes of picnics, fairs and flirtations. You can see them on the top floor. He rose fast, and in 1799 he became First Court Painter. But in the winter of 1792 he had fallen seriously ill, and the illness left him completely deaf. He was in his late forties. From then on, his art turns inwards and darker. In 1799 he published the Caprichos, a set of etchings mocking superstition, the clergy and human folly.

In 1800 he painted The Family of Charles the Fourth, the royal family in a row, glittering with silk and medals. People still ask whether it is a satire. Almost certainly not; the family seem to have been pleased with it. But the faces are so unflattering that it is hard not to see it as a portrait of a dynasty about to fall. Look for Goya himself, in the shadows at the left, standing at a canvas. It is a direct quotation of Las Meninas.

Nearby are the two Majas, one clothed and one naked, painted for the king's chief minister and the queen's favourite, Manuel Godoy, who kept the nude in a private cabinet. When Godoy fell, the Inquisition seized the pictures and summoned Goya to explain himself.

Then came the war. In May 1808 the people of Madrid rose against the French garrison, and on the night of the third of May the French shot hundreds of prisoners in reprisal. Six years later, when the French had gone, Goya painted both days, and you will find them on the ground floor. The Third of May is the one everyone knows: a man in a white shirt, arms flung wide like a crucifixion, in front of a firing squad who have no faces. It is the first modern image of war, and nearly every war picture since, from Manet to Picasso, owes it something.

Peace did not bring freedom. The restored king, Ferdinand the Seventh, abolished the liberal constitution and brought back absolute rule and the Inquisition. In 1819 Goya, now in his seventies, bought a house outside Madrid, by the river Manzanares, known as the Quinta del Sordo, the Deaf Man's Villa. And there, between about 1820 and 1823, he covered the walls of two rooms, the dining room downstairs and a sitting room upstairs, with fourteen paintings made for nobody but himself. They are known as the Black Paintings.

Fifty years later they were peeled off the walls and transferred to canvas, and they now hang together in Room sixty-seven. When you go in, try to imagine the house. You are in an old man's dining room. On one wall, Saturn devours his own son, his eyes wide with terror. On another, a crowd of hideous faces gathers around a goat. Elsewhere two men beat each other with clubs, sunk to their knees in the earth, unable to run. And in one tall, almost empty canvas, a small dog's head looks up out of a sea of ochre at something we cannot see. Many people find that one the most moving picture in the building.

In 1824 Goya left Spain for exile in Bordeaux, where he died four years later, at eighty-two. One of his very last paintings, The Milkmaid of Bordeaux, hangs nearby: a young woman painted in soft, broken strokes of blue and green, gentle and calm, half a century before Impressionism. It is a strange and tender ending.

Ten. Listening with your eyes

If you love music, the Prado has a hidden thread for you. In Bosch's hell, a sinner lies pinned under a lute with a line of music written on his backside; it has been transcribed and performed. In Titian's Andrians, on the island where a river of wine flows, a sheet of music lies in the foreground with a French canon on it, about drinking and drinking again, attributed to Adrian Willaert, the Flemish master of music at Saint Mark's in Venice. In Titian's Venus with an Organist, the musician twists around at the keyboard to stare at the goddess, caught between hearing and sight, the old philosophical argument about which of the senses leads us to beauty. And in Bruegel's Triumph of Death, in the bottom right-hand corner, a pair of lovers go on singing from their part-book, while a skeleton plays along behind them.

Eleven. How to look, in five habits

First, choose. You cannot see the Prado in an afternoon, and nobody expects you to. Pick perhaps twenty pictures and give each one real time. Walk briskly past the rest without guilt.

Second, look before you read. Give a painting a full minute before you look at the label or the guide. Ask what you notice first, and where your eye goes next. Then read, and look again.

Third, use three distances. From across the room, see the composition, the big shapes. At normal distance, see the people and the story. Close up, see the paint. With Velázquez and Titian especially, the close-up and the far view are two different pictures.

Fourth, ask where you are standing. Many of these paintings were made for a particular place: above a door, at the top of an altar, on the wall of a dining room. Prince Baltasar Carlos's pony looks oddly fat because it was meant to be seen from below. Las Meninas puts you in the king's place. The Black Paintings were meant to be lived with.

Fifth, follow the conversation. The collection is, above all, painters looking at other painters. Rubens copies Titian. Velázquez answers both. Goya quotes Velázquez. When something reminds you of a picture you saw an hour ago, it is probably not a coincidence.

Twelve. Afterwards

When you come out, the Retiro park is right behind the museum, a few minutes' walk up the hill. It was once the garden of Philip the Fourth's palace of the Buen Retiro, for which Velázquez painted The Surrender of Breda and the royal portraits on horseback. Almost all of that palace is gone, but two of its buildings survive, and one of them, the Hall of Realms, just north of the museum, is being converted into new galleries for the Prado. If you have time on another day, the Reina Sofía, ten minutes' walk south, finishes the story: Picasso's Guernica, painted in 1937, is the direct descendant of Goya's Third of May.

That is all you need. The rest is looking. Enjoy the Prado.
